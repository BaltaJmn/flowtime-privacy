The 50/10 rule in three steps

Work for 50

Pick one task and work on it for 50 minutes with your phone out of reach. A soft chime tells you when time is up, so you can stop watching the clock.

Rest for 10

Stand up and step away from the screen for the whole 10 minutes. Do not borrow time from the break to finish one more thought.

Repeat, then rest longer

Start the next block on a new task, or on a new part of the same one. After two or three blocks, take a longer break.

Is 50/10 right for you?

Choose 50/10 for deep work

Use it when you read, write, code or solve problems, and 25 minutes ends just as you get going.

Choose 25/5 to get started

Use it when you are tired, the job is small or you keep putting it off. A short block is easy to begin.

Not sure? Test both

Try each for one full day and keep the one you actually finish, not the one that sounds more serious.

No fixed bell

Some days your focus ends at 35 minutes and some days it runs past 50. FlowTime mode counts up instead: you stop when your attention fades, and the break fits the time you worked. It is the same idea as 50/10 without the fixed bell: a long run of work, then a rest in proportion.

More on the Flowtime technique

What is the 50/10 rule for studying?

The 50/10 rule is a study method with blocks of 50 minutes of focused work followed by a 10 minute break. It is the longer version of the Pomodoro technique, which uses 25 minutes of work and a 5 minute break.

The ratio is the same, five to one, but each block lasts twice as long. That helps with tasks that take a while to warm up, such as following a proof, writing an essay or tracing a bug, where a 25 minute bell tends to ring right when you finally get in.

The rule suits students with long texts to read, essays to draft or problem sets to work through, and anyone whose work needs a long run, such as programming or design. It is also a good fit if you find 25 minutes too short and the constant restarts annoying.

Is 50/10 better than 25/5?

Neither is better: 50/10 suits deep work, and 25/5 suits starting and low-energy days. Use 50/10 for a chapter, an essay, a problem set or code you have to hold in your head.

Use 25/5 when you are tired, stressed by a pile of work or putting something off, because 25 minutes feels easy to begin. Many people use both in one day: 50/10 in the morning while they are fresh, 25/5 after lunch when energy dips. In the FlowTime app you can set your own times, so 50/10 is only one option.

A simple test: at minute 40, do you still know what you are doing? If you are rereading the same line, the block is too long for today. Shorten it instead of forcing it.

How many 50/10 blocks can you do in a day?

Four to six blocks is a solid day for most people. That is around three to five hours of real focus, since the breaks do not count. Start with three and add one more when you finish them without a fight.

Count only the blocks you finish with your phone away, and write the task for each one before you press start. When you reach your number, stop: being able to repeat tomorrow matters more than one heroic day.

A day could look like this: two blocks in the morning, a longer break, one or two after lunch and a last one in the evening if you still have energy. Put the hardest subject in the first block, while you are fresh.

What should you do in the 10 minute break, and when do you need a longer one?

Move, drink some water and look at something far away; leave the phone alone. Walk around, stretch, refill your glass or look out of a window. Ten minutes is enough for a snack or a quick errand, but not for scrolling a feed and coming back on time.

Take a longer break of 20 to 30 minutes after two or three blocks, or sooner if you catch yourself rereading without taking anything in. Eat, go outside or lie down with your eyes closed, then start the next block on a different task.

Keep the end of the break firm. When the second chime plays, put the phone down and start; a break with no end tends to swallow the next hour.

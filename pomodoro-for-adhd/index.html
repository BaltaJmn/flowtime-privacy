Getting started with a timer

Start small

Press start on 25/5 and promise yourself only the first block. A clear end makes starting feel less heavy.

Write the step first

Before you press start, write one concrete step, such as solve problems 1 to 5 instead of study maths. You skip the first minutes of deciding what to do.

One alarm only

Silence the phone and keep this tab open. The soft chime marks the end of the block, so you do not have to watch the time.

More on the Flowtime technique

Does the Pomodoro technique work for ADHD?

It helps some people and not others, so think of it as an experiment. Some people with ADHD find that a short timed block makes starting easier, because the task has a clear end and the countdown makes time visible. Others find the countdown stressful, or lose a good run of work when the bell rings.

Try it for a week, one task a day. Notice how easy it was to begin, how long you stayed and how you felt afterwards, then keep what helps and drop what does not. None of this is medical advice, and a timer does not replace support from a professional.

If a timer makes you more anxious, that is a useful answer too. Count up with FlowTime mode, or skip timing for a day and write only a list of steps.

Can you use shorter blocks, like 10/3 or 15/5?

Yes. Many people shorten the block to 10 minutes of work and a 3 minute break, or to 15 and 5, when 25 minutes feels too long to begin. A short block you finish is worth more than a long one you dread.

The web timer on this page has three modes: 25/5, 50/10 and FlowTime. For other times, the FlowTime app lets you set your own Pomodoro times. Here you can get a similar effect in FlowTime mode: press Start break after 10 minutes and the break will be 5.

Other changes are small: keep a short list of steps in sight, take a longer break after a hard block, or pick the easiest task for the first block so the day starts moving.

Can body doubling help you study?

Some people find that working next to someone else makes it easier to start and harder to drift off. That is body doubling: another person is there, doing their own work, in the same room or on a call. You do not need to talk or study the same subject.

If you cannot meet, a shared timer does a similar job from a distance. Coop, in the FlowTime app, is a private room where up to 8 people share one timer. A library, a study group or a friend on a video call work too.

If it helps, say your task out loud in one line before you start, then stay quiet until the break.

What if a block goes badly, or you forget to stop?

Do not count a bad block as failed. Write one line about where you stopped, take the break anyway and start again with something smaller: 10 minutes, one step. A rough block that ends with a note for next time still moved the work forward. Then ask what made it hard: were you tired, hungry, in a noisy room, or facing a task that was too big? Change that one thing for the next block.

If you get absorbed and lose track of time, 25/5 and 50/10 help because the chime pulls you out to drink water, eat and move. Before you stop, write the next step so it is easy to restart. In FlowTime mode, glance at the counter now and then, and stop when you catch yourself rereading the same line.

Stopping early is allowed. Some days the honest result is a single 10 minute block, and that is still a day with work in it.

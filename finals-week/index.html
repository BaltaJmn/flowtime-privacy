Make the schedule work

Sleep is part of the plan

Sleep helps your memory hold on to what you studied. A 1 a.m. session usually costs you the next morning.

Test yourself, don't reread

Practice questions and recall stick better than rereading notes, and they show you what you still miss.

Study with someone

A friend on the same timer makes it harder to quit early. That's what Coop is for.

How do you plan a finals week study schedule?

Start with a one-page calendar of the whole week: write each exam on its date, then count the study days you have before each one. Give the exam that scares you most the earliest free day, while you still have time to fix what you find.

Then split each day into three or four blocks and give each block one job: a subject, a type of practice or a topic list. Leave one block per day empty as a buffer, because something always runs over. If two exams are close together, alternate their subjects across days so neither goes cold. The day plan on this page shows what a single day can look like.

How many hours should you study for finals?

Most students do well with 4 to 6 hours of focused study a day, and up to 8 on the days just before the hardest exams. That means focused time, not hours spent at a desk with your phone beside you.

Count only the minutes when the timer is running. A day with five hours of real focus beats a ten-hour day of half attention, and you can repeat it tomorrow. If you have many exams, add days instead of lengthening each one; past eight hours, most people get less and less back from every extra hour. The FlowTime app keeps statistics of your focused time, so you can check the number at night instead of guessing.

How do you study for finals when there is too much material?

Triage first: sort every topic into strong, shaky and missing, and spend most of your time on the shaky ones. Strong topics need a quick check, and missing ones may not deserve a whole day if they carry few marks.

For each topic, write a one-page summary from memory, then compare it with your notes and fill the gaps. Do old exam papers or practice questions as early as you can; they show what the exam really asks and where you lose marks. Keep a list of your mistakes and reread it every evening. It is short, personal and more useful than another chapter.

What should you do the night before an exam?

Do a light review, pack your bag and go to bed at a normal time. Do not start new topics tonight and do not cram until 3 a.m.: a rested head recalls more than a tired one that saw one more page.

Read your one-page summaries and your mistakes list for no more than an hour. Check the exam time, the room and what you must bring: ID, pens, calculator, water. Eat a proper dinner, put the phone away and set two alarms. If worries keep you awake, write them on paper and leave them on the desk. A short walk beforehand helps many people sleep.

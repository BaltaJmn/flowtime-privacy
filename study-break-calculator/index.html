How long should a study break be?

After 1 hour

Take about 12 minutes, one fifth of the time you studied. Stand up, drink some water and look out of a window.

After 2 hours

Take about 24 minutes. It is long enough to eat something and get outside, and short enough to pick up the thread afterwards.

After 3 hours

Take 30 minutes, which is where the rule stops. A longer break tends to make it harder to get back to work. Eat properly and go for a walk.

How often should you take breaks when studying?

Every 25 to 50 minutes

A short break of 5 to 10 minutes after each block of focus is a pattern most people can keep. The 25/5 and 50/10 modes follow it.

Every couple of hours, a longer one

Add a break of 20 to 30 minutes to eat, move and reset before the next stretch of study.

Sooner if you drift

If you reread the same line, check your phone without noticing or cannot say what you just read, rest now instead of pushing for ten more minutes.

More on the Flowtime technique

How do you work out the length of a study break?

Take one fifth of the time you studied, round it, and keep it between 5 and 30 minutes. Fifty minutes of study gives a 10 minute break, 60 gives 12, 90 gives 18, 120 gives 24 and 180 gives 30. The calculator above does the sum for you.

The minimum of 5 minutes stops a short session from earning a break too small to be useful. The maximum of 30 keeps the rest from growing so long that you lose the thread. The rule is a starting point: if you finish a hard block drained, take a few extra minutes. A 20 minute session between classes earns the 5 minute minimum, which is enough to stand up and drink some water.

Are short breaks or long breaks better?

You need both: short breaks keep your attention from fading, and long breaks let you eat, move and reset. Short breaks of 5 to 10 minutes suit the middle of a task, and a long break suits the end of one.

A simple day could be three blocks of 50 minutes with 10 minutes between them, followed by a break of 30 minutes. Then you start again with a different task, which also gives your attention something fresh.

If you study for four hours or more, split the day into two stretches with a proper meal between them. A long break is easier to take when it already has a purpose.

What should you do on a study break?

Move, drink some water and rest your eyes. Walk around, stretch your neck and back, refill your glass and look at something far away for a minute or two. Eat something if you are hungry. Water matters more than it seems: put a glass on the desk before you start, so you do not wander to the kitchen and get pulled into something else.

Scrolling is not a break: your eyes stay on a screen and your attention stays busy. The same goes for starting a video. Leave the phone in another room while you rest, and use the time for something that needs no decisions, such as a stretch, a song or a few slow breaths.

When you only have five minutes, stand up, stretch, drink and look away from the screen. Do not start anything that needs a decision, because it will follow you back to the desk.

How do you get back to work after a break?

Start with the easiest next step, and start the timer before you sit down. Before each break, write one line about what you will do first when you return; reading that line is your restart.

If it is hard to begin, promise yourself five minutes. Once you are moving, most of the pull to put it off fades. If you still cannot focus after a proper break, switch to a different task instead of staring at the same one.

Give the break a clear end. Before you leave the desk, decide the time you will return, such as 4:30, and keep that time; a rest with no end tends to swallow the next hour.
